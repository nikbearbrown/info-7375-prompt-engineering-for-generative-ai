Agentic patterns · Reasoning

Reflection

Execute, evaluate against a rubric, revise.

This deck covers one pattern: Reflection. It shows the shape of the pattern, the way it fails, and the question that decides whether to add it.

Conceptual illustration

Where this pattern sits

Twenty patterns in three families

Sources: Design of Agentic Systems with Case Studies, Chapter 3. Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents". The Forward Future Loop Library (85 loops).

On entering: an empty map. The set of decks covers twenty patterns, grouped by the question each family answers.

Step 1: reasoning patterns. These describe how one agent orders its own thinking and tool calls.

Step 2: workflow patterns. These describe how several model calls are wired together.

Step 3: loop patterns. These are grouped by the check that keeps a long run honest.

Step 4: this deck is Reflection, the fourth reasoning pattern.

The claim

Reflection adds a self-audit to any pattern. The audit is only as reliable as its rubric.

The model that did the work also grades it. That is cheap to add and it works on top of other patterns. It also means the grade can only be trusted when the rubric asks for things that can be checked from outside the model.

Part one

The shape

Execute, evaluate against a defined rubric, optionally revise

Three slides: the cycle, the chapter's example rubric applied to a draft, and where the pattern sits relative to the others.

Conceptual illustration

Structure

The model grades its own output before it answers

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy", Reflection.

On entering: three empty places.

Step 1: the model executes the task and produces an output.

Step 2: it evaluates that output against a defined rubric. The rubric is written down. It is not the model's general impression.

Step 3: if a criterion fails, it revises and evaluates again. Revision is optional. It happens only on a failed criterion.

Step 4: when every criterion passes, the output is returned. Notice who did the grading: the same model that did the work.

Where it sits

Reflection is a layer, not a replacement

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy", Reflection.

On entering: two execution patterns, ReAct and Plan-and-Execute. Each has an empty place above it.

Step 1: Reflection goes on top of ReAct. ReAct still does the work. Reflection audits what comes out.

Step 2: the same layer goes on top of Plan-and-Execute.

Step 3: the chapter's word is composable. Reflection is a post-processing layer. It does not replace the pattern underneath.

Part two

How it fails

Sycophantic self-evaluation: the grader approves of its own work

The failure comes from the same property that made the pattern cheap. The grader and the author are the same model.

Conceptual illustration

The mechanism

A self-evaluation can pass on polish, not on the criteria

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy", Reflection.

On entering: three steps, taken from the chapter. This is the chapter's explanation of the failure. It is an account of a mechanism, not a measurement.

Step 1: fine-tuning with human feedback rewards outputs that human evaluators rate positively.

Step 2: evaluators tend to rate confident, fluent, well-structured responses highly, even when the content is incomplete or subtly wrong.

Step 3: so when the model evaluates its own output, it may find the output satisfactory because it has those surface properties, and not because the rubric criteria were met.

Conceptual illustration

The defence

Some rubric items can be checked from outside. Some can't.

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy", Reflection. Both rubric items are the chapter's examples.

On entering: two rubric items, both from the chapter.

Step 1: "Does this response cite a source?" The model says yes. Anyone can look. The citation is there or it is not. This item is checkable.

Step 2: "Is this response insightful?" The model says yes. There is nothing outside the model to compare that answer with. This item is not checkable.

Step 3: the chapter's conclusion: Reflection is most reliable when the rubric is externally verifiable.

Part three

When to use it

Quality-critical output, and a rubric someone else could check

One question from the chapter's decision framework, then a checklist.

Conceptual illustration

The decision

One question decides whether to add a Reflection layer

Source: Chapter 3, "The Decision Framework", question 4 and the summary table.

On entering: the fourth question in the chapter's decision framework.

Step 1: does the task require self-evaluation? If the output is quality-critical and the rubric is verifiable, add a Reflection layer. The chapter derives this question from the gap between execution quality and output quality in complex tasks.

Step 2: the row from the chapter's table. Best for quality-critical work with a verifiable rubric. Breaks on sycophantic self-evaluation.

Step 3: the pair to tell apart. Reflection is a layer that audits output. An execution pattern, such as ReAct or Plan-and-Execute, sets the order of thinking and tool calls. You choose an execution pattern first, then decide whether to add Reflection.

Conceptual illustration

Checklist

Six checks before you add Reflection

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy", Reflection, and "The Decision Framework", question 4.

Steps 1 to 3: the conditions the chapter gives for Reflection: quality-critical output, a defined rubric, and items that can be checked from outside.

Steps 4 to 6: the conditions under which it breaks. An opinion item gives the self-evaluation nothing to be wrong against. A pass on surface polish is the failure the chapter names. And Reflection on its own does no work: it needs an execution pattern underneath.

Takeaway

Reflection is only as reliable as its rubric. Write criteria someone else could check.

What would change this advice: an evaluator that is independent of the author is a different pattern, covered later in the set under "Maker is not the checker". Next in the set: Tree-of-Thoughts, which explores several reasoning paths before it commits to one.
