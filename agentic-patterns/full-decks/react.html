Agentic patterns · Reasoning

ReAct

Reason, act, observe, repeat.

This deck covers one pattern: ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. It shows the shape of the pattern, the way it fails, and the questions that decide whether to use it.

Conceptual illustration

Where this pattern sits

Twenty patterns in three families

Sources: Design of Agentic Systems with Case Studies, Chapter 3. Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents". The Forward Future Loop Library (85 loops).

On entering: an empty map. The set of decks covers twenty patterns, grouped by the question each family answers.

Step 1: reasoning patterns. These describe how one agent orders its own thinking and tool calls.

Step 2: workflow patterns. These describe how several model calls are wired together.

Step 3: loop patterns. These are grouped by the check that keeps a long run honest.

Step 4: this deck is ReAct, the first reasoning pattern.

The claim

ReAct can change course after every tool call. It pays for that with context.

Both halves come from the same mechanism. Every observation stays in the context so the next thought can use it. That is what makes the pattern adaptive, and it is also what fills the window.

Part one

The shape

Thought, action, observation, in one growing context

Three slides: the cycle, a real trace of two cycles, and why the observation matters.

Conceptual illustration

Structure

One cycle adds a thought, an action and an observation

Source: Chapter 3, "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy". Pattern proposed by Yao et al., 2022.

On entering: three empty places. A reasoning pattern is a rule for which operations run in what order.

Step 1: the thought is reasoning in plain language.

Step 2: the action is a structured tool call.

Step 3: the observation is the value the tool really returned.

Step 4: the cycle repeats. The next thought is written after the model reads the observation, so each step is conditioned on the last real result.

A trace

The second thought depends on the first observation

Source: Chapter 3, "ReAct Deep Dive", Structure.

On entering: the task arrives and the model writes its first thought.

Step 1: it calls a tool and reads the result. That is one full cycle.

Step 2: the second thought uses the warranty length from the first observation. The model could not have written it before the tool returned.

Step 3: the second cycle completes. Note that all six lines are still in the context.

Conceptual illustration

Why it helps

An observation stops a wrong guess from compounding

Source: Chapter 3, "ReAct Deep Dive", Logic.

On entering: a chain of thoughts with no tool calls.

Step 1: one wrong guess early, and every later thought builds on it.

Step 2: the same wrong guess inside a ReAct run.

Step 3: the observation puts a real value into the context before the next thought. The chapter's wording: the model is shown what the system said, so it cannot fabricate it. This is the chapter's argument, drawn as a picture. It is not a measurement.

Part two

How it fails

Context saturation: the window fills and early evidence fades

The failure comes from the same property that made the pattern useful. Nothing is summarized or removed, so the context only grows.

Illustrative data

The case: a billing-support agent

A customer asks a phone company's chatbot why her bill went up $34

  • The agent. A support chatbot built on ReAct. The book calls it Helios.
  • Its record. In its pilot it answered 91% of billing questions on the first contact.
  • Its tools. Look up the account, the bill and the device payment plan. Every answer goes into its context.
  • Its window. 8,192 tokens, shared by every tool result, every thought and the reply.

Source: Chapter 3, "The Scenario". Paraphrased.

Before the numbers, the story. A customer opens a support chat with her phone company: her bill went up by $34 this month, and she was told last month that the problem was fixed. The company's support agent is a ReAct system that did well in its pilot. It answers by calling lookup tools one at a time, and every result stays in its context window, which holds 8,192 tokens. The next slide follows that window as the tool results arrive.

Illustrative data

The billing agent, call by call

Three tool calls used 7,700 of 8,192 tokens

Source: Chapter 3, "The Scenario". Token counts are the chapter's; totals are added in code.

On entering: an empty 8,192-token window. Helios is a support agent built on ReAct. A customer asks why her bill rose by $34.

Step 1: the account summary returns 1,800 tokens.

Step 2: the billing detail returns 2,200 more. The total is 4,000.

Step 3: two reasoning steps add 600 tokens of scratchpad. The total is 4,600.

Step 4: the device payment schedule returns 3,100. The total is 7,700, which leaves 492 tokens for the rest of the reasoning and the reply.

Computed

The mechanism

Nothing is deleted. Each early token gets a smaller share.

Source: Chapter 3, "The Mechanism Behind Context Saturation". Share computed as 1 / n.

On entering: attention weights are normalized, so they add up to 1.0 across every token in the context.

Step 1: with 50 tokens, an equal share is 1 in 50, which is 2 percent.

Step 2: grow the context to 7,700 tokens. The same token's equal share is 1 in 7,700, about 0.013 percent. The red mark is still there, but it is too thin to see.

Step 3: equal shares are a simplification, because real attention is not uniform. The direction is the point. The early observation is still present. It carries less weight when the reply is written.

Illustrative data

The failure signature

The reply was fluent and half right

Source: Chapter 3, "The Scenario". The reply text is a paraphrase.

On entering: the bill rose by $34.

Step 1: there were two causes of $17 each: an expired promotional discount, and a device plan that moved to an unsubsidized tier.

Step 2: the reply explains the discount and issues a credit.

Step 3: it never mentions the device plan. No error was raised. The postmortem found accurate tool outputs, valid reasoning steps and no hallucination. A check on fluency or customer sentiment would not catch this. The customer came back three times.

Part three

When to use it

Count the steps, and ask whether the plan must change mid-task

The decision questions come straight from the failure modes. Name the failure you are accepting before you build.

Conceptual illustration

The decision

Two questions decide whether ReAct fits

Source: Chapter 3, "The Decision Framework", questions 1 and 2.

On entering: two questions, asked in order.

Step 1: how many tool calls? Five or fewer, ReAct is viable. More than five, saturation becomes a design risk. The threshold is the chapter's rule of thumb for an 8K window, not a constant.

Step 2: must the next step use what the last step actually returned? If yes, you need ReAct. If the steps are fixed by the task, Plan-and-Execute is safe.

Step 3: the pair to tell apart. ReAct chooses each step after reading the last observation. Plan-and-Execute commits to every step before any tool has run.

Computed

The cost of waiting

Five independent calls: ReAct waits for each one

Source: Chapter 3, "ReWOO". Both totals are computed in code from 5 calls at 300 ms.

On entering: five tool calls at 300 milliseconds each.

Step 1: ReAct runs them one at a time, because each thought waits for the previous observation. Five times 300 is 1,500 milliseconds.

Step 2: ReWOO plans all five first and runs them together. The total is the slowest call, 300 milliseconds.

Step 3: the gain holds only when no call needs another call's output. When calls depend on each other, the waiting is the price of adapting.

Checklist

Six checks before you choose ReAct

Source: Chapter 3, "ReAct Deep Dive", Outcome.

Steps 1 to 3: the conditions under which the chapter says ReAct is correct.

Steps 4 to 6: the conditions under which it breaks. If any of these describes your task, look at Plan-and-Execute or ReWOO, or keep the evidence outside the context.

Takeaway

ReAct buys adaptation with context. Count the steps before you choose it.

What would change this advice: a much larger window, or a design that summarizes old observations, moves the threshold. It does not remove the mechanism. Next in the set: Plan-and-Execute, which commits to the plan first and fails in the opposite way.
