Plan before building

Tighten the documents until two readers would build the same thing.

This deck covers one loop pattern: plan before building. It shows the shape of the pattern, the three loops that use it, the way it fails, and when to pick it.

Conceptual illustration

Where this pattern sits

Twenty patterns in three families

Sources: Design of Agentic Systems with Case Studies, Chapter 3. Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents". The Forward Future Loop Library (85 loops).

On entering: an empty map. The set of decks covers twenty patterns, grouped by the question each family answers.

Step 1: reasoning patterns. These describe how one agent orders its own thinking and tool calls.

Step 2: workflow patterns. These describe how several model calls are wired together.

Step 3: loop patterns. These are grouped by the check that keeps a long run honest.

Step 4: this deck is Plan before building, the seventh loop pattern.

The claim

A vague idea is not ready for a long agent run. Tighten the plan until independent readers would build the same thing, then stop.

A loop is a prompt plus a real check plus a stop rule. In this pattern the check is a pair of independent readers, and the stop rule is that they would build the same thing.

Say this plainly: the ten loop patterns are a grouping made by reading a catalog of 85 loops. The grouping is a judgment, and none of these loops has been tested on real runs for this deck.

Part one

The shape

Interview or review, tighten the documents, test them on two readers

Two slides: the cycle with its check and stop rule, then the stop rule played out round by round.

Conceptual illustration

Structure

Ask, tighten, and stop when two readers agree

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7. Loops from the Forward Future Loop Library.

On entering: the problem. The idea is too vague to hand to an agent for a long run.

Step 1: interview or review. Keep asking until the open questions have answers.

Step 2: tighten the documents. Each answer is written into the plan.

Step 3: the check. Would independent readers build the same thing from these documents?

Step 4: if not, go round again. If yes, stop and hand the plan over.

Step 5: a loop is a prompt, a real check and a stop rule. Here the check is the two readers, and the stop rule is that their builds match.

Illustrative data

The stop rule at work

Round by round, the two builds converge

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7, and loop 043. The six decisions and their answers are illustrative; the match counts are computed in code.

On entering: six decisions a build needs. Two readers get the same documents. Neither sees the other's build.

Step 1: round one. The plan settles two decisions. The readers fill in the other four themselves, and they fill them in differently. The builds match on two of six.

Step 2: round two. Two more answers are written into the plan. The builds match on four of six.

Step 3: round three. Nothing is left open. The builds match on six of six, so the loop stops.

Step 4: this is loop 043's test: two independent reviewers derive substantially the same build from the project documents. The decisions and answers here are made up to show the test. They are not from a real run.

Part two

The loops

Three loops, from a vague idea down to one ticket

This pattern has three child loops. Each works at a different scale: a vague idea, a new project, one ticket.

Conceptual illustration

The children

Three loops tighten a plan at three scales

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7. Loops from the Forward Future Loop Library. Watch-for lines are a reading of each loop, not the catalog's wording.

Step 1: loop 035, the Goal Forge loop. It interviews you, then writes SPEC.md, what to build, and GOAL.md, how Codex should run and verify it. Use it when a vague idea is not ready for a long autonomous run. It settles scope, checks and safety limits before tokens are spent.

Step 2: loop 043, the prepare-a-new-project loop. It closes documentation gaps until two independent reviewers derive substantially the same build. Use it before building, when the early docs leave decisions open.

Step 3: loop 082, the spec dev-review loop. It writes the planning artifacts for one ticket: requirements, edge cases, task plan and review rubric. It applies review gates, logs every round, and stops at approval or a clear blocker.

Step 4: what to watch for in each. Loop 035 is specific to Codex and is only as good as your answers. Loop 043 must not pad documents or invent requirements. Loop 082 implements nothing itself, and its round caps and review tooling must be adapted to your project. These cautions are a reading of the loops, not the catalog's own text.

Part three

How it fails

The readers agree, and nobody decided anything

The stop rule is agreement. So the failure is agreement reached the wrong way.

Conceptual illustration

The failure

Forced agreement is how this loop fails

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7, "Needs" and "Fails when". The three routes are illustrative.

On entering: one open decision, and three ways the loop can deal with it.

Step 1: the route the pattern intends. A person answers the question, the answer goes into the plan, and the readers agree because it was decided.

Step 2: an invented requirement. No one is asked. A requirement is written so the readers will agree. They do, and nobody decided it.

Step 3: padding. No one is asked. The documents get longer. The decision is still open, and the plan only looks finished.

Step 4: the loop tree's two lines. It needs someone to answer the questions. It fails when documents are padded or requirements are invented to force agreement.

Part four

When to use it

Before a long run, when the idea is still vague

Pick the pattern from the problem you have, then the child loop closest to your subject. Then be honest about what this pattern does not do.

Conceptual illustration

The decision

Use it when the idea is too vague to run, and know its limit

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7 and "How to use the tree". The contrast with Plan-and-Execute is this set's.

On entering: start from the problem you have.

Step 1: if the idea is too vague to hand to an agent for a long run, this is the pattern.

Step 2: then pick the child closest to your subject: a vague idea, a new project, or one ticket.

Step 3: the limit. The loop tree calls this one of the two weakest patterns on its own, because it stops work rather than improving it. That is a judgment from reading the loops, not from running them.

Step 4: the pair to tell apart. In this loop pattern the planning documents are tightened before any run starts. In the Plan-and-Execute reasoning pattern, one agent writes its own step plan inside a run. The contrast is made by this set of decks. Neither source draws it.

Conceptual illustration

Checklist

Six checks before you run a plan-before-building loop

Source: the loop tree, pattern 7 and "How to use the tree", and the entries for loops 043 and 082.

Steps 1 to 3: what has to be true before you start: the idea is too vague to run, someone can answer the questions, and two independent readers will test the documents.

Steps 4 to 6: the signs it has failed: padded documents, an invented requirement, or an expectation that this loop builds something. It only gets the plan ready.

Takeaway

Tighten the plan until two readers would build the same thing. Then stop planning and hand it over.

What would change this advice: results from real runs. This deck is a reading of the loops, not a test of them. Next in the set: Standing watch, the pattern for recurring work that should happen without anyone remembering.
