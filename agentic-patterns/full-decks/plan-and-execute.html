Agentic patterns · Reasoning

Plan-and-Execute

Write the whole plan first. Then run it.

This deck covers one pattern: Plan-and-Execute. It shows the shape of the pattern, the way it fails, and the questions that decide whether to use it.

Conceptual illustration

Where this pattern sits

Twenty patterns in three families

Sources: Design of Agentic Systems with Case Studies, Chapter 3. Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents". The Forward Future Loop Library (85 loops).

On entering: an empty map. The set of decks covers twenty patterns, grouped by the question each family answers.

Step 1: reasoning patterns. These describe how one agent orders its own thinking and tool calls.

Step 2: workflow patterns. These describe how several model calls are wired together.

Step 3: loop patterns. These are grouped by the check that keeps a long run honest.

Step 4: this deck is Plan-and-Execute, the second reasoning pattern.

The claim

Plan-and-Execute commits to every step before any tool has run. That makes the plan easy to audit, and it makes failures silent.

Both halves come from the same commitment. Because the plan exists before execution, someone can read it, and independent steps can run in parallel. Because the plan was written before any tool answered, it has no way to react when a tool returns something unexpected.

Part one

The shape

Plan every step, then run the steps in order

Two slides: the two phases, and what committing to a plan buys you.

Conceptual illustration

Structure

The plan is finished before the first tool is called

Source: Chapter 3, "Plan-and-Execute Deep Dive", Structure.

On entering: two phases, kept apart on purpose.

Step 1: planning. The model sees the full task. No tool has run.

Step 2: it writes a structured plan: ordered steps, each with a name for its output. The letters here are placeholders for those output names.

Step 3: execution. The executor follows the plan in order and substitutes each earlier output into the step that needs it.

Step 4: the executor does not re-evaluate whether a step is still correct, given what the earlier steps returned. Hold on to that sentence. Part two is built on it.

Conceptual illustration

What committing buys

The plan can be read before anything runs

Source: Chapter 3, "Plan-and-Execute Deep Dive", Logic and Implementation.

On entering: the plan exists and nothing has run yet. That gap is the useful part.

Step 1: the chapter calls this the natural place for a Human Decision Node. A domain expert reads the plan before execution.

Steps 2 to 4: the three things the chapter says to verify before proceeding: the order by business rules, which steps lean on unreliable tools, and whether the risky steps need a re-planning trigger.

Step 5: the second gain. Steps that do not depend on each other's data can run in parallel.

Part two

How it fails

Stale-plan execution: a step fails and the plan carries on

The failure is the mirror image of the benefit. The pattern gains auditability and parallelism by committing before any tool is called. That commitment is what makes it fail silently.

Conceptual illustration

The Happy Path Assumption

The plan is written before any tool has answered

Source: Chapter 3, "The Happy Path Assumption".

On entering: a four-step plan.

Step 1: when the planner wrote it, no tool had been called. It reasoned from what tool calls typically return, so every step assumes a normal result.

Step 2: for the first two steps the assumption holds.

Step 3: step 3 returns something outside what the planner expected: an error, an empty response or an unexpected schema.

Step 4: the executor has no mechanism to account for it. It stores the unexpected value and moves on to the next step, which was designed assuming the one before it succeeded.

Illustrative data

The warranty claim: step 3 fails

Step 3 failed. Steps 4 to 8 ran anyway.

Source: Chapter 3, "The Failure Cases", failure case 2, and "Plan-and-Execute Deep Dive", Implementation. Step count computed as 8 − 3.

On entering: the chapter's 8-step warranty claim task, planned in full.

Step 1: steps 1 and 2 run as planned.

Step 2: at step 3 the inventory check fails. The executor puts the error value into step 3's output and continues. The code comment says it plainly: error or not, the result goes into context.

Step 3: steps 4 to 8 run on that value. That is five of the eight steps.

Step 4: the final output is a resolution email and a CRM log entry. It is structurally complete and factually wrong. In the chapter's words, no exception was thrown. This is the companion notebook's failure case as the chapter describes it. I did not run the notebook.

How often

Eight steps at 5% each: about one run in three hits a failure

Source: Chapter 3, "The Decision Framework", question 3. Computed as 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.

On entering: suppose each tool call fails 5 percent of the time.

Step 1: the chance that at least one call fails grows with every step: 5 percent for one step, 33.7 percent for eight. The formula treats the calls as independent.

Step 2: one minus 0.95 to the eighth power is 0.337. The chapter rounds this to 34 percent. Without a re-planning trigger, each of those runs ends as a silent wrong output.

Step 3: at 1 or 2 percent per call the picture changes: 7.7 and 14.9 percent. The 5 percent threshold is a conservative heuristic. Use the failure rates you observe in your own tools.

Computed

Where the failure lands

The earlier the failure, the more of the plan it corrupts

Source: Chapter 3, "Chapter Exercise", exercise 2. Counts computed as 8 − the failing step.

On entering: the same 8-step plan, twice.

Step 1: the failure at step 3, the inventory check. Five later steps run on corrupted input.

Step 2: move the failure to step 6, the fraud check. Two later steps run on corrupted input.

Step 3: the chapter states this as a predicted relationship for the reader to test in the notebook, not as a measured result. The counts here assume every later step runs, which is what the plain executor does.

Part three

When to use it

Long, fixed tasks with reliable tools, or a trigger for when a tool fails

The defense, the decision questions and a checklist.

Conceptual illustration

The defense

A re-planning trigger stops the run at the failed step

Source: Chapter 3, "Chapter Exercise", exercise 3, and "The Decision Framework", question 3.

On entering: the same failure at step 3. This time the later steps have not run.

Step 1: when a step returns an error, the executor calls the planner again.

Step 2: it passes two things: the remaining steps and the current context.

Step 3: the planner writes a new plan for the remaining steps.

Step 4: the chapter sets this as an exercise and leaves two questions open. It does not claim the re-planner always finds a valid recovery path. Its decision framework says only that, when any tool fails more than 5 percent of the time, Plan-and-Execute without a re-planning trigger is unsafe.

Conceptual illustration

The decision

Three questions decide whether Plan-and-Execute fits

Source: Chapter 3, "The Decision Framework", questions 1 to 3, and "The Five Patterns: A Structural Taxonomy".

On entering: three questions, asked in order.

Step 1: how many tool calls? More than five, and a pattern that plans first is the safer choice.

Step 2: must the next step use what the last step actually returned? If the steps are fixed by the task, Plan-and-Execute is safe. If not, you need ReAct.

Step 3: how often do the tools fail? If any tool fails more than 5 percent of the time, the pattern needs a re-planning trigger.

Step 4: the pair to tell apart. Both plan first. Plan-and-Execute runs the steps in order and lets a step use an earlier output. ReWOO plans every tool call up front and runs the calls in parallel or in one batch.

Checklist

Seven checks before you choose Plan-and-Execute

Source: Chapter 3, "Plan-and-Execute Deep Dive", Outcome.

Steps 1 to 4: the conditions under which the chapter says Plan-and-Execute is correct.

Steps 5 to 7: the conditions under which it breaks. If any of these describes your task, add a re-planning trigger or look at ReAct.

Takeaway

Plan-and-Execute buys an auditable plan by committing early. Decide what happens when a tool fails before you run it.

What would change this advice: tools that almost never fail, or a re-planning trigger on the risky steps. Next in the set: ReWOO, which also plans first, and then runs every tool call at once.
