# Final project brief — honest tool trials for education

**DRAFT 2026-10-07. Items in [brackets] are mine to confirm or fill in. Nothing here is submitted.**

## Executive summary

I am building a library of learning materials that shows students and teachers how the
AI and design tools in their classrooms actually perform. The tools are Figma, Claude,
Google's tools such as NotebookLM, Microsoft's, OpenAI's, and others as I find them.
Each piece is a book chapter, workshop or short film.

The test is the same for every tool. I use it on a real education problem, honestly,
and film what happened, including what worked and what didn't. The viewer then judges
for themselves whether it's useful to them.

My judgment decides which tools get evaluated at all. That judgment comes from years of
experience and from serving on Northeastern's educational committees, which shape the
tools the university uses. I am a human being with a perspective, and the project says
so.

I publish every evaluation, whether the tool passes or is dropped, with no filter. The
materials are public, on YouTube and elsewhere, so anyone who thinks I dropped a tool
wrongly can say so and ask me to revisit it. I want other people's perspectives added
to mine.

The first two audiences are students doing real work where they are learning, and
teachers evaluating real learning rather than just the finished artifact.
Administrators who want to log learning, and the companies that make these tools, come
later.

My own course is the first test case. Students use the tools on real assignments and
give feedback while I write them, and I use the tools as the teacher to design and
evaluate the work.

## The seven answers

### 1. What is it?
The library described above. When it is done there is a set of dated, public films, each
trial of one tool on one real education task, with the problems shown, plus the book
chapters and workshops that sit around them.

### 2. Why would anyone care — what makes it special?
A teacher or student deciding whether to use a tool mostly sees vendor demos, feature
lists, or opinion with no task behind it. This project puts the tool on a real
assignment, from the student's side and from the teacher's side, and shows the failures
next to the wins. Three things set it apart:

- **Real tasks, two seats.** The same tool is seen by the student doing the work and the
  teacher judging whether learning happened, not only whether an artifact was produced.
- **Dropped tools are published.** Most coverage only shows what the author endorses.
  Here a dropped tool gets its film too, and the comments are an open invitation to
  argue for revisiting it.
- **The judge is named.** The verdict comes from a person with a stated perspective and
  a stated basis, not an anonymous "best tools" list.

### 3. Who opens it, and what do they do with it?
A teacher deciding whether to bring a tool into a course, and a student deciding whether
a tool will help or hurt their learning. In the first sixty seconds they see the tool
used on a real assignment and the first thing that went wrong. They then decide for
themselves, and can answer in the comments. Later readers are administrators and the
companies' education teams.

### 4. What does it prove about me?
That I can evaluate a tool with evidence instead of enthusiasm: I pick a real task, run
it honestly, show the failure, and let the result stand. A reviewer could fairly
conclude that I can run an honest trial and report it, and that I am willing to publish
the result when it goes against a tool I might have expected to like. They could **not**
conclude that any tool is good in general — each film is one trial, dated.

Evidence: the films themselves, the published drops, and the public comments and
revisions that follow.

### 5. What is in, what is out, what comes first?
- **In:** one tool, one real education task, one film at a time; students first, then
  teachers evaluating learning; every outcome published.
- **Out, for now:** administrators' and companies' perspectives; any tool I have not used
  myself; any claim that a verdict holds for everyone or for long.
- **Out, always:** promoting a tool I don't believe in.
- **First:** the film you are reading about is diary entry 1, dated 2026-10-07, on the state
  of the job-search project (see `DIARY-ENTRY-2026-10-07.md`). It has run when the film
  exists and says what has not been done. Later entries repeat the form.

### 6. What could sink it?
Tools change faster than films do. A verdict that was fair in October can be unfair, or
simply wrong, by January, and a stale "dropped" film does a company and its viewers real
harm. Plan: date and version-stamp every film, say on screen what would change my
verdict, re-run a trial when a tool changes materially, and keep the comment invitation
open.

A second risk is credibility. A single judge, free accounts and my own committee roles
can read as endorsement. Plan: disclose every free account or access I was given, in the
film and in `SOURCES.md`.

### 7. Why this project?
It is a mix. **Skills I already have:** evaluating tools for educational use through
Northeastern's committees, building Figma for Educational AI (a book, workshops and 36
films), building Claude-based course material, and producing films with Brutalist. The
evidence for those is already public. **Skills that are new to me in this format:**
filmed, honest trials of Google, Microsoft and OpenAI tools, and testing whether a tool
shows real learning rather than just a finished artifact. [Confirm which of the Google,
