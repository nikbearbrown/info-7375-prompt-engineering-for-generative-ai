## 2. Relevance is not entailment

**The case.** Under v3, three postings were `keep`, the strongest decision the filter makes. Each one's claim "the posting does not rule out sponsorship" was labelled **supported**, with this evidence:

| Posting | The "supporting" sentence |
|---|---|
| Recruiting Coordinator (Fixed Term), Ramp | "We will **sponsor** you for **relocation** if you need to move for this role." |
| Strategic Account Executive, Space Force, Databricks | "...secure executive **sponsorship**, and deliver beyond customer expectations." |
| AI Account Executive, Glass, Ramp | "...to earn executive **sponsorship** and create urgency." |

Each sentence is **relevant**: it contains the word the rule looks for. None of them **entails** the claim. Relocation money and an executive's backing say nothing about a visa.

v1–v3 accepted any sentence with `sponsor`, `visa` or `work authorization`. That made the label measure relevance and call it support.

**The same flaw in the other direction.** Under v1, held-out row 41 ("This position is not eligible for immigration sponsorship") was labelled **supported**: the sentence mentions sponsorship, and no excluding phrase in the list matched it. The sentence entails the **opposite** of the claim.

**The fix (v4).** A sponsorship claim is supported only by a sentence that has both a sponsor word and a visa word (`visa`, `immigration`, `H-1B`, `work authorization`), and no negation. Row 41 is now **contradicted** by the new phrase "not eligible for immigration sponsorship" (v2c). The three relocation and executive sentences are now **insufficient**. Tests: `test_v4_relevant_sentence_is_not_entailment`, `test_v2c_immigration_sponsorship_phrase_is_contradicted`.

## 3. The abstention rule, and a counterexample

**The rule (v4).** The agent says **"I cannot tell"**, instead of keep or reject, when any of these holds:
- the description is under 200 characters;
- the location is empty;
- the posting would be kept, but no sentence states that visa sponsorship is available.

A real case where abstaining is right: row 8, *UX Quantitative Research Intern (USA)*, Pinterest. The posting says "applicants must be currently legally authorized to work in the United States on a full-time basis." Whether an F-1 student on OPT counts as "currently authorized" is a legal question the text does not settle. v4 marks it **insufficient** rather than guessing either way. I labelled it Y.

**A counterexample: the rule produces a confident wrong answer.** I constructed this one; it is **not** from the data.

> "Visa sponsorship is available **for senior roles only**."

This sentence has a sponsor word and a visa word and no negation, so v4 labels the sponsorship claim **supported** and the decision is `keep`. For a new-grad, non-senior role, the sentence entails the opposite.

The rule checks the **form** of a sentence: which words it contains and whether it is negated. It does not check what the sentence limits. `test_v4_known_limit_a_qualified_yes_is_read_as_yes` pins this wrong behaviour on purpose, so a future fix has to change it knowingly.

**A second limit, from my labels.** Row 46 ("Must be planning on graduating in 2028") reaches Mira as "cannot tell — sponsorship". The rule abstains on the claim it can read and is silent on the one it cannot: a graduation date. "Cannot tell" about sponsorship does not mean that everything else was checked.
